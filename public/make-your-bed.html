Make Your Bed

Admiral William H. McRaven — Little Things That Can Change Your Life
10 lessons Six months of SEAL training One speech that became a book
Timeless reflection episode — coming soon

In 2014, Admiral McRaven stood before the graduating class at the University of Texas and made a strange promise: if you want to change the world, start off by making your bed.

The speech drew on the ten hardest lessons of Navy SEAL training. It became one of the most-watched commencement addresses ever given — and then a small book with a big argument: it’s the little things that change everything.

University of Texas at Austin — Commencement, 2014
I

Start Your Day with a Task Completed

Every morning in basic SEAL training, instructors inspected the beds. Square corners. Tight blanket. Pillow centered under the headboard.

It seemed ridiculous — aspiring warriors graded on hospital corners. But the wisdom was quiet: making your bed is the first task of the day, done right. One small pride. One small sense of order. Momentum before the world gets a vote.

And if the day goes badly — and some days will — you come home to a bed that is made. A made bed is proof that tomorrow can be better.

If you can’t do the little things right, you will never do the big things right. Admiral William H. McRaven
II

You Can’t Go It Alone

Trainees paddle small rubber boats through pounding California surf. Every wave threatens to flip the boat. No one gets through alone — not the strongest, not the most stubborn.

Everyone paddles in rhythm, everyone pulls their weight, and the coxswain’s job is to keep the crew in sync — not to paddle hardest.

Life is the same boat. Find people to help you paddle — and be someone worth having in the boat when their wave comes.

You can’t change the world alone — you will need some help. Admiral William H. McRaven
III

Measure a Person by the Size of Their Heart

The best boat crew in McRaven’s class wasn’t the tall one. It was the “munchkin crew” — nobody over five-foot-five, little flippers, mocked by everyone.

They outpaddled, outran, and outswam every other crew.

SEAL training was a great equalizer: nothing mattered but your will to succeed. Not your color, not your background, not your education, not your social status.

Measure a person by the size of their heart, not the size of their flippers. Admiral William H. McRaven
IV

Life’s Not Fair — Drive On

Fail a uniform inspection — sometimes for no reason at all — and you ran fully clothed into the surf, then rolled in the sand until every inch of you was covered. A “sugar cookie.” Wet and sandy the rest of the day.

The instructors weren’t looking for a perfect uniform. They were looking for the student who couldn’t accept that effort doesn’t always get rewarded — the one who complains, who blames, who breaks when things aren’t fair.

Sometimes you do everything right and still end up a sugar cookie. Get over it. Keep moving forward.

Sometimes, no matter how well you prepare or how well you perform, you still end up as a sugar cookie. Admiral William H. McRaven
V

Failure Can Make You Stronger

Fall short on any event and your name went on a list. That evening you got “the circus” — two extra hours of calisthenics designed to break you.

McRaven and his swim buddy made the list constantly. But all those circuses had a side effect no one advertised: they were getting stronger. By the end of training, the pair that failed the most was among the fastest in the water.

The pain of failure builds the capacity that comfort never will. Don’t fear the circus.

Life is filled with circuses. You will fail. You will likely fail often. It will be painful. But if you persevere, it will make you stronger. Admiral William H. McRaven
VI

You Must Dare Greatly

The obstacle course record stood for years — until one student went down the “slide for life” head first. Riskier. Faster. The record fell.

The safe way was proven. It was also the reason the record never moved.

Playing it safe feels responsible. Over a lifetime, it’s the quiet way to guarantee you never find your edge. At some point you have to go head first.

Slide down the obstacle head first. Admiral William H. McRaven
VII

Stand Up to the Bullies

Before the night swim off San Clemente Island — water thick with sharks — the instructors gave simple guidance: if one circles you, stand your ground. Don’t swim away. Don’t act afraid. And if it charges, punch it in the snout.

Bullies are all the same — in the water, in the schoolyard, in the office, in whole countries. They feed on fear and retreat.

The lesson isn’t about sharks. It’s that the moment you show you won’t be moved, most predators look for easier prey.

If you hope to complete the swim, you will have to deal with the sharks. Admiral William H. McRaven
VIII

Rise to the Occasion

The ship-attack dive is the darkest part of training — literally. Under an enemy hull at night, no light reaches you. You can’t see your hands. The machinery hums above you and it’s easy to get disoriented, easy to panic.

And that’s exactly where the mission gets done. At the darkest moment of the mission is the time to be calm, composed — your very best.

Everyone hits a keel moment eventually: the diagnosis, the loss, the failure that goes all the way down. What you’ve practiced in small dark moments is what shows up in the big one.

At the darkest moment of the mission, is the time when you must be calm, composed — when all your tactical skills, your physical power and all your inner strength must be brought to bear. Admiral William H. McRaven
IX

Give People Hope

Hell Week, Wednesday night: the class stands neck-deep in the freezing mud of the Tijuana flats. The instructors offer a deal — if just five men quit, everyone else can come out of the mud and warm up by the fire.

Eight hours of cold left. Some are ready to take the deal. Then one voice starts singing. Badly. Then two. Then the whole class — and somehow the mud feels a little warmer, the dawn a little closer.

One person, singing, changed what the whole group believed was possible. Hope is contagious — and it only takes one carrier.

If I have learned anything in my time traveling the world, it is the power of hope. The power of one person — Washington, Lincoln, King, Mandela and even a young girl from Pakistan, Malala — one person can change the world by giving people hope. Admiral William H. McRaven
X

Never, Ever Quit

In the center of the training compound hangs a brass bell. Ring it, and the suffering ends instantly. No more 5 a.m. swims. No more cold. No more circuses. All you have to do is quit — publicly, with one pull of the rope.

The ones who made it through weren’t always the strongest or the fastest. They were the ones who simply refused to touch the bell.

Every hard thing you’ll ever do hangs its own bell nearby. Quitting is always available, always easier, always ringing softly. The whole game is deciding — in advance — that it isn’t an option.

If you want to change the world, don’t ever, ever ring the bell. Admiral William H. McRaven
