Explain, Challenge, Revise

One scholar–builder pair. Two weeks. A prototype that makes AI advice inspectable.

Research → design

Verification Is Not Knowledge emphasizes being able to explain and defend a claim when challenged. Cosmos Grants funds autonomy-focused prototypes. This example translates those themes into a design question: does an interface that connects claims to evidence and invites revision make an AI answer easier to inspect than a plain-answer interface?

What the pair would build

A small browser prototype with two versions of the same non-sensitive claim-evaluation task. Both use the same curated public or synthetic source packet and fixed AI answer. The plain version shows the answer and sources. The guided version first asks for the participant’s initial view, connects claims to source passages, invites a challenge, and asks what changed and why.

Example task: assess whether a fictional community program should expand based on attendance, repeat participation and participant feedback. Include a deliberately unsupported inference in the fixed answer, such as treating registrations as attendance. Clearly debrief any planted error; this is a test fixture, not a factual claim about Cosmos.

People & existing work

Scholar: defines justified acceptance/rejection and reviews the task, answer key and failure cases. Builder: implements the two variants and fixes usability issues. Jack: owns the brief, scheduling, scope control, check-ins and final decision memo. No one is committed.

First review Priori’s existing interface and study work. Steven Molotnikov and Cathy Fang are public-source review leads, not assigned participants. Reuse or adapt relevant work with agreement; do not create a parallel project if it duplicates an existing effort. See the evidence-linked shortlist.

What we would inspect

Record whether people locate relevant source passages, identify the unsupported inference, and explain why they accept or revise a claim. Note confusion, time burden and where the interface obstructs rather than helps. Use a small reviewed rubric and retain examples of errors. More disagreement is not automatically better judgment.

These are proposed usability observations. A small walkthrough cannot establish causal effects or lasting autonomy gains. Participant sessions require an agreed consent, data-handling and applicable research-review approach. No sensitive personal decisions or data are needed.

Tradeoff & decision afterward

The guided version adds effort. It may support reflection or merely frustrate users. Continue to another scoped iteration only if the prototype works, an expert sees a distinct contribution, and observations justify it. Revise if instructions confuse or burden participants. Stop if the question duplicates existing work, the pair cannot be staffed, or the test adds no useful evidence.
