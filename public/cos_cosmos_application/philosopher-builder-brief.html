Helping people question AI advice

This is one project I’d help you organize: pair a scholar from the Cosmos community with a developer for two weeks to build a tool that helps people examine the reasoning behind an AI answer.

Why this project?

Cosmos already brings thinkers and builders together. This pilot would give a pair a focused question to work on: can a tool help people examine the evidence behind AI advice and decide whether to accept it? Jessica Hullman’s October 30 essay offers a starting point, asking why understanding and accountability matter alongside correct answers.

What we would build

The pair would build two versions of a small tool using the same sources and AI answer. One would show the answer and sources directly. The other would first ask people for their own view, connect the answer’s claims to the sources, and ask what they would challenge or change, and why.

For example, someone could assess whether a fictional community program should expand. The AI answer could deliberately confuse registrations with attendance. We would explain that planted error afterward and look at whether the tool helped people notice and question it.

Who would do what?

The scholar would help define sound reasoning for the task and review the examples. The developer would build both versions. I’d handle the brief, scheduling, check-ins and final recommendation. Before choosing a pair, I’d review Priori’s existing work with Steven Molotnikov and Cathy Fang in mind as people to learn from. They have not agreed to participate. We should adapt relevant work where possible and change the question if this would duplicate it.

How the two weeks would run

WhenWhat we would do
Days 1–2Review existing work, choose the task and agree on the time and resources available.
Days 3–5Build both versions and review the sources and example answer together.
Days 6–8If agreed, invite 4–6 volunteers to try the tool. Otherwise, ask an expert to walk through it.
Days 9–10Demonstrate the tool, discuss what we learned and recommend whether to continue, change direction or stop.

What would make it worth continuing?

We would look at whether people find relevant evidence, notice the unsupported claim and explain their reasoning. We would also note confusion and the time the extra steps take. The tool might help people think more carefully, or it might just frustrate them. Disagreeing with AI more often would not, by itself, show better judgment.

I’d deliver a working demonstration, a record of what helped or failed, and a recommendation for the next step. A small trial would not prove a lasting improvement in autonomy. Any volunteer sessions would need appropriate consent, care with their information and any research review required. We would use public or made-up examples.

The first decision

If this seems useful, I’d ask you to suggest someone at Cosmos who could help shape it. Within two working days of that conversation, I’d bring back a suggested pair, a time estimate and a plan. We would agree on the work and resources before inviting anyone.
