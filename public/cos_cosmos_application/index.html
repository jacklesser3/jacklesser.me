Chief of Staff search · Proposed review criteria

Review every application against the same questions.

I’d agree on the criteria with you first, then read each application and pull out the evidence that helps us decide who to meet.

What I’d look for

  • Judgment: Can the candidate explain a difficult decision, the alternatives they considered and what they learned?
  • Execution: Have they taken an unclear idea through to a useful result, coordinated the people involved and followed through?
  • Clear writing: Can they explain a complicated issue, make a recommendation and support it without losing the reader?
  • Engagement with Cosmos’s work: Do they understand the questions you’re working on, including where they disagree or would challenge an assumption?
  • Working relationships: Do their examples show discretion, thoughtful preparation and the ability to work across different people and priorities?

I’d assess the work and reasoning they demonstrate. A familiar employer or an unconventional background would not settle the question. Where an application lacks evidence, I’d flag what we need to ask rather than treat the gap as proof that the candidate cannot do the job.

What you would receive

I’d keep a record of every application reviewed, with brief source-linked notes against the agreed criteria. The review brief would recommend whom to interview, explain why, and separate promising candidates from cases that need a second look. You would have the original applications alongside my recommendations.

I’d also prepare interview questions for the gaps that matter and a suggested work exercise tied to the role. You and the agreed reviewers would make the selection decisions.

What we need to agree

Which qualities matter most, who joins the review, what access I should have and when you want the first brief. We should also agree how to handle candidate information and any conflicts of interest. If I’m still an applicant for this role, I should not review competing applications.

Philosopher-builder pilot · Proposal

Helping people question AI advice.

Cosmos already brings philosophers and builders into the same community. I’d like to help a pair turn one of those conversations into something people can try.

Why start here?

Jessica Hullman’s October 30 essay ↗ asks what we lose when we can verify an answer but cannot explain or defend it. Cosmos’s grants program ↗ supports tools that help people think for themselves. That suggests a useful question for a small project: can we make it easier for someone to examine the evidence behind an AI answer and decide whether it holds up?

What the pair would build

A scholar and a developer would spend two weeks building a simple tool around one task. Someone using it would read a short set of sources, record their own view and then see an AI answer. The tool would connect the answer’s claims to the sources and ask the person what they would challenge or change, and why.

The pair would also build a simpler version that shows the same answer and sources without those prompts. Comparing the two would help us see whether the extra steps make the reasoning easier to follow or just make the task more frustrating.

What I’d need from you

First, I’d ask whether this is a useful question for Cosmos and who you’d suggest to help shape it. Within two working days of that conversation, I’d bring back a suggested pair, an estimate of the time involved and a delivery plan. We would agree on the work and resources before inviting anyone.

Build on what already exists

I’d begin with Priori, which already helps people examine choices behind AI responses. Cosmos describes a collaboration between Steven Molotnikov and Cathy Fang, so understanding their work should come before starting anything new. They are people to learn from, not confirmed participants. If this idea repeats what they are already doing, I’d recommend adapting it or choosing a different question.

What I’d deliver

The scholar would help define what good reasoning looks like in the task. The developer would build the tool. I’d organize the work, keep the project manageable and make sure both have what they need. At the end, I’d deliver a working demonstration, a record of what confused or helped people, and a recommendation on whether to keep going, change the approach or stop.

  1. Days 1–2: Review existing work, choose the task and agree on the time and resources available.
  2. Days 3–5: Build both versions and review the sources and example answer together.
  3. Days 6–8: If agreed, invite 4–6 volunteers to try the tool and explain their thinking. Otherwise, ask an expert to walk through it.
  4. Days 9–10: Demonstrate the tool, discuss what we learned and recommend a next step.

The extra prompts might help people think more carefully, or they might simply get in the way. This project would give us an early look at that tradeoff. It would not establish a lasting improvement in autonomy. Any sessions with volunteers would need appropriate consent, care with their information and any research review required. We would use public or made-up examples rather than sensitive personal decisions.

Metric definitions
Change: later counter minus earlier counter for the same post, metric and method. Divide by actual elapsed time for velocity. Preserve negative corrections and flag them.
Fair comparisons: same platform, metric and post age. Use medians and label sample sizes. A launch outlier should not define routine performance.
Missing data: null and a reason. Do not interpolate failed collections or turn blocked access into zero. Exact early-life performance is unavailable for historical posts unless supplied by a legitimate historical source.
Review and reliability rules
Retry temporary errors with backoff; honor rate limits and a daily spending ceiling. Pause an adapter on authentication failure and surface the issue. Deletion requires an explicit platform response or manual confirmation.
Use idempotent run IDs, immutable observations and versioned imports. Record the API/UI method; do not calculate a delta across incompatible sources. Store timestamps in UTC, display in America/New_York; retain the original snapshot’s Chicago date basis.
